# Parte 3. Formas y choques: the lesson sheets

The design each lesson of part 3 is written from, under the approved [curriculum](../tutorial-curriculum.md) (§5, part 3) and [decision 0021](../decisions/0021-a-new-tutorial.md). Abbreviations and conventions are the curriculum's §2: **E** (Ericson, the part's lead), **L1**, **L2**, **G**, **G2**, **RTR**, **MF**, **MA**, **GPP**. Every formula is in our conventions: planes are [**n** | _d_] with **n**·**x** + _d_ = 0 (E writes **n**·_X_ = _d_: flip the sign of _d_), column vectors, right-handed, +Y up, SI units, _h_ = 1/60 s. Book text is never copied; chapter and section titles are cited.

Two rules run through the whole part (curriculum, part 3): **every test is shown twice**, first the equation, then the picture that rejects early (RTR); and **every lesson has a "Se reduce a…" line** that lights one arrow of E's reduction graph on the part's introduction page.

## The part sheet

**What the reader can do at the end.** Describe a shape as a mesh with a consistent winding and normals that survive any transform. Write, derive and test the basic queries: the closest point (on a segment, a box, a triangle), a ray against a plane, a sphere, a box and a triangle, the separating axis, a moving sphere against a sphere and a box. Reduce a new query to one already solved (closest point, ray against a grown shape, intervals on an axis). Pick the floor under the mouse. Build a broad phase, check it against brute force and measure it. Keep a ball out of walls and let it slide; stop a fast ball from tunnelling; design geometry that floats cannot break. Play a maze generated from a seed.

**The introduction page.** E's framing (E ch. 1–2): collision detection answers _whether_, _when_ and _where_ two things touch, at rising cost, plus distance, depth and contact points. Collision shapes are simple proxies, not the drawn meshes (G2 §14.3.1: gameplay object, collider and mesh are three things). Two phases: broad (who might touch), narrow (do they). And E's reduction graph, with each arrow lit as its lesson is done: closest point → spheres and capsules → walls; substitution → rays → slabs; support point → separating axis → Minkowski → moving tests; floats → robust geometry.

**The closing game: _el laberinto_ (3.17).** A maze of 12 × 12 cells of 2 m, generated from a seed by depth-first backtracking (a spanning tree, so always solvable) plus a few loops. The ball of 2.8, driven by actions, slides along walls (3.14). Coins are placed from the seed (2.13). Part 1's turrets stand at some junctions as sentries: a turret sees the ball when it is inside its cone (1.7) and a ray walked through the grid reaches it before a wall; after half a second in sight, the hit costs 5 s of the timer. A dash (one press, 2 s cooldown) launches the ball fast for a few steps and uses the swept test (3.15), so it never passes through a wall: it is how to cross a turret's line. A right click drops a breadcrumb on the floor point under the mouse (3.5); clicking a turret shows its view cone (object picking, 3.9). **Win:** reach the exit (the cell farthest from the start by path length) with at least _c_ coins before the timer ends; score = steps left + 100 per coin. **Lose:** the timer reaches zero. Restart keeps the seed; `maze.seed` changes it. It exercises the closest point, the grid broad phase, rays and grid walks, the swept sphere, merged boxes (robustness), the frame bar and seeded chance.

**How the project's code evolves.** Names keep their meaning once taught (0021).

- **3.5:** `geom.ts` (new): `Ray` {origin, direction}, `Plane` {normal, d}, `signedDistance`, `rayPlane`. `pick.ts` (new): `rayThroughPixel`, `pickFloor`. A picked point enters the simulation as a command carrying the world point.
- **3.14:** `collide.ts` (new): `Box` {center, half} (axis-aligned), `Contact` {point, normal, depth} (normal from the obstacle toward the ball), `closestPointOnBox`, `sphereBox`, `pushOut`, `slide`, `collideBall`. `grid.ts` (new): `Grid` (hashed uniform grid), `insert`, `near`. Walls get `group` and `material` (an id). `profile.ts` (new, view only): `mark`, the frame bar.
- **3.17:** `maze.ts` (new): `generateMaze`, `addLoops`, `wallsOf` (merged runs), `walkGrid`. `collide.ts` grows `sweepBallBox`. `game.ts` gains the mode `maze`.
- **Forward:** `closestPointOnBox`, `sphereBox` and `Grid` are reused by 4.6's toy engine; `Contact` is extended (not renamed) by 4.6's `ParticleContact`; `slide` is recalled in 4.3 as the contact with _e_ = 0 and no friction.

**Shared lab-kit pieces this part needs (§4.2).** A point and a segment with closest-point markers; a plane with its normal and its half-space shaded; Voronoi regions of a box and a triangle; intervals on an axis (the shadows of the separating axis, the slabs) and a time bar (the separating axis in time); a swept shape with its time of impact; a 2D convex-polygon editor with the Minkowski sum and difference; a grid overlay with occupancy and test counters; a BVH tree view linked to its boxes; a **brute-force reference** that runs beside a clever test and counts disagreements (E §2.7.1); the telemetry box; the naive-against-correct toggle; perturbations "Float32" (`Math.fround`) and "far from the origin"; time controls with a step slider; the seed box; the frame bar (3.13).

## The lessons

### 3.1 Mallas: vértices, triángulos, sentido (C)

- **Question:** ¿De qué está hecha una forma, y cómo se sabe qué cara mira afuera?
- **Before:** 1.6 (cross product, signed area, ORIENT2D and ORIENT3D, the triple product as a signed volume); 1.8 (the determinant's sign). The reader has drawn three.js boxes since 1.3 without looking inside one.
- **Leads:** L1 3.1 (vertex and index lists, closed meshes, Euler's formula stated, winding). **Also:** RTR 16.2–16.4 (meshes as they really arrive: triangulation, orientation by flood fill, the signed volume, T-vertices; a Möbius strip cannot be oriented); L2 §5.5.1–5.5.2 (a vertex is a tuple of attributes; rasterization's tie rules); G2 §11.3.6, §11.5.1.1 (indexed lists, backface culling); E §3.8, §12.1–12.3 (soups against welded meshes, 2-manifolds, cracks).
- **Approach:** E's frame: the representation decides which questions can be asked; a polygon soup has no inside, a closed and consistently wound mesh has one. L1 gives the minimal definitions, RTR the realistic failures, 1.6 the sign test that decides orientation. Rejected: the API-first framing (strips, fans, buffers, the post-transform cache), which belongs to 8.1. **Hook:** the maze needs walls; the reader lists a wall's triangles by hand, writes one backwards, and that face vanishes (three.js culls back faces). Which side is out, and who decides?
- **The mathematics:** (1) A mesh is a vertex list plus index triplets; (_i_, _j_, _k_) equals its cyclic rotations, and (_k_, _j_, _i_) is the other side. (2) The front is counterclockwise seen from outside: (_B_ − _A_) × (_C_ − _A_) points out (right-hand rule, 1.6). (3) Backface culling is the sign of the projected signed area: ORIENT2D on the screen (RTR 19.3: equal to the view-space dot test). (4) Closed: every edge in exactly two triangles; consistent: each shared edge traversed in opposite directions by its two triangles; orientation repaired by flood fill. (5) Signed volume _V_ = (1/6) Σ _A_·(_B_ × _C_), one tetrahedron per triangle to the origin (1.6's triple product): positive iff the normals point out. Picture: a centred cube is six pyramids of volume 1/6. (6) Counting: 3*F* = 2*E* for a closed triangle mesh; with Euler's _V_ − _E_ + _F_ = 2 (stated; a proof sketch in "Más a fondo"), _E_ = 3*V* − 6 and _F_ = 2*V* − 4. **Worked example:** a unit cube centred at the origin: 8 vertices, 12 triangles, 18 edges, 8 − 18 + 12 = 2, signed volume 1. Flip one face (two triangles): 4 inconsistent edges, volume 2/3. Flip all: −1.
- **The live figure:** the cube with numbered vertices and its index list; clicking a triangle shows its triplet and normal; a flip toggle per triangle; a "repair" button runs the flood fill (naive: as given; correct: oriented). Second scene: a ring (genus 1). Perturbation: duplicate one face's vertices, as a careless export does, then weld with a tolerance. Panel: _V_, _E_, _F_, _V_ − _E_ + _F_, open edges, inconsistent edges, signed volume. **Expected:** cube 8 / 18 / 12 / 2 / 0 / 0 / 1.000; one face flipped: inconsistent 4, volume 0.667; after repair 1.000; duplicated face: open edges 8 (Euler still reads 2: two disks, a good surprise); welded: back to 8 / 18 / 12; ring: _V_ − _E_ + _F_ = 0.
- **Traps:** three.js's `BoxGeometry` has 24 vertices, not 8: a hard edge needs one normal per face, so positions repeat (L1, L2). Inconsistent winding breaks culling and normals. A T-junction (a vertex on a neighbour's edge) cracks the surface (E Fig. 12.9, RTR 16.8): the crack a ball falls through in 3.16. Collinear extra vertices fool convexity tests (E Fig. 3.18). Capsule _Del modelo al motor_: a model exported with a mirrored axis comes in inside out (det −1); import with a rotation (det +1), never a mirror.
- **Exercises:** (1) count _V_, _E_, _F_ for an octahedron (6, 12, 8) and an icosahedron (12, 30, 20). (2) Prove _E_ = 3*V* − 6 for a closed triangle mesh like a sphere (hint: eliminate _F_ between 3*F* = 2*E* and Euler). (3) In a closed mesh, the fewest triangles that can meet at a vertex (3, L1 Ex. 3.1).
- **Our angle:** orientation is 1.6's sign carried across a surface; the Möbius strip is the surface where the flood fill must fail (RTR 16.3.2), planted for 10.6, and the ring's 0 for 10.7. Se reduce a: a triangle's orientation is ORIENT3D. Manifold's levels are built from boxes, not meshes, so this lesson's failures never reach its colliders (3.14 says why).
- **Sources:** L1 3.1; RTR 16.2–16.4, 19.3; L2 §5.5.1–5.5.2; G2 §11.3.6, §11.5.1.1; E §3.8, §12.1–12.3, §12.6. Free: Keenan Crane, [_Discrete Differential Geometry_](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~kmcrane/Projects/DDG/) (Euler's formula); the [three.js manual](https://threejs.org/manual/) (BufferGeometry).
- **Reuses:** nothing from the pilot or the archive.
- **Open:** whether the origin-independence of the signed volume is a "Más a fondo" or an exercise.

### 3.2 Normales (C)

- **Question:** ¿Hacia dónde mira una superficie, y qué le pasa a esa dirección al estirarla?
- **Before:** 3.1 (winding, the face normal); 1.10 (points and directions; the inverse transpose stated there as a warning); 1.9 (inverse, transpose, orthogonal matrices).
- **Leads:** L1 3.2 (the gradient, the triangle normal, the transformation law with two proofs). **Also:** RTR 16.3.4 (vertex normals, angle weighting, the crease angle), 4.1.7 (asserts the inverse transpose; we prove it), 5.3.1 (renormalize after interpolating); L2 §7.2 (interpolated normals shorten); E §12.4.2 (Newell's normal for a polygon that is not quite planar).
- **Approach:** L1's framing: a normal is defined by what it is perpendicular to, so it must transform to stay perpendicular. Derived from that condition (L2's method), not asserted (RTR). Intuition from L1: a gradient's components are reciprocal distances. **Hook:** 1.12's turret, its barrel stretched ×3 along its length to become 4.14's cannon; with its normals multiplied by the same matrix, its shading tilts wrong.
- **The mathematics:** (1) The triangle normal (_B_ − _A_) × (_C_ − _A_) has length twice the area, so summing raw face normals around a vertex is area weighting for free (L1, ours). (2) Vertex normals: averages; RTR's argument that angle weighting does not depend on how a polygon was triangulated; the crease angle. (3) For an implicit surface _f_ = 0, the normal is ∇*f* (an ellipsoid), planted for 10.2. (4) **The law:** want **n**′·(**Mt**) = 0 for every **t** with **n**·**t** = 0. Then **M**ᵀ**n**′ is perpendicular to every such **t**, so it is parallel to **n**, so **n**′ ∥ (**M**⁻¹)ᵀ**n**. Three lines. (5) For a rotation, (**M**⁻¹)ᵀ = **M**: why engines often get away without it. (6) L1's second proof: a cross-product normal transforms by the adjugate, det(**M**)(**M**⁻¹)ᵀ; the determinant's sign survives normalization, so a mirror flips that normal and the winding together. (7) The average of two unit normals at angle θ has length cos(θ/2): renormalize. **Worked example:** a slope with tangent **t** = (1, 1, 0) and normal **n** = (−1, 1, 0)/√2, stretched by **M** = diag(2, 1, 1). **Mt** = (2, 1, 0). The naive **Mn** ∝ (−2, 1, 0) makes 126,9° with **Mt**; (**M**⁻¹)ᵀ**n** ∝ (−1/2, 1, 0) makes 90,0°.
- **The live figure:** the stretched barrel beside a 2D slope; a scale slider along _x_; the naive **Mn** (red) and (**M**⁻¹)ᵀ**n** (green), with the toggle driving the lighting. Second panel, one knob and two failures: a crease-angle slider on a cylinder (faceted at one end, mushy caps at the other; the caps separate below 90°), and RTR's "split the quad" toggle (the plain average moves, the angle-weighted one does not). Panel: scale, angle between normal and stretched tangent (naive, correct), det **M**, winding flipped. **Expected:** at scale 2, 126,9° and 90,0°; at 1, both 90,0°; at −1, the correct normal stays perpendicular and "winding: flipped".
- **Traps:** translation never touches normals; the inverse transpose does not keep length: renormalize after transforming and after interpolating; non-uniform scale is when it matters. three.js's `normalMatrix` is exactly the inverse transpose of the model-view 3×3, and three.js flips the front face when the world matrix's determinant is negative (the L1 reader checked `three.module.js`; recheck against the installed version). Capsule _Rigor numérico_: Newell's normal for a bent quad.
- **Exercises:** (1) Scale a sphere into an ellipsoid (_a_, _b_, _c_) and check that the inverse transpose of the sphere's normal is parallel to ∇(*x*²/*a*² + *y*²/*b*² + *z*²/*c*²) (hint: both are (_x_/*a*², _y_/*b*², _z_/*c*²) up to length). (2) Prove the cos(θ/2). (3) An edge shared by two triangles is concave iff the far vertex of the second lies in front of the first's plane (E §12.5.3); classify a small mesh's edges.
- **Our angle:** the "what transforms how" table on the symbols page gains its **Normal** row here (§2); normals as antivectors in a "Más a fondo" (L1 4.1.6); the mirror returns as orientation in 10.6, the gradient as "down" on a planet in 10.3. Manifold computes no normals of its own (three.js draws them): "Manifold todavía no lo tiene".
- **Sources:** L1 3.2, 4.1.6; RTR 16.3.4, 4.1.7, 5.3.1; L2 §7.2, §7.5; E §12.4.2, §12.5.3. Free: Dunn and Parberry, [_3D Math Primer_](https://gamemath.com/book/).
- **Reuses:** nothing.
- **Open:** whether the adjugate proof is main path or "Más a fondo" (it carries the mirror, so main path is suggested).

### 3.3 Rectas, rayos y segmentos (C)

- **Question:** ¿Cómo se describe un camino recto, y a qué distancia pasa de un punto?
- **Before:** 1.5 (projection and rejection; the extreme point); 1.6 (cross product as area).
- **Leads:** L1 3.3 (the parametric line, the point–line distance two ways, two lines). **Also:** E §3.5 (one form, three ranges of _t_), §5.1.2 (project, then clamp); RTR 22.2 (a unit direction makes _t_ a distance; a maximum length prunes); MA §3.3.14 (the time of closest approach, sign corrected); G §5.6.1, G2 §14.3.7.1 (a ray cast returns a _t_).
- **Approach:** E's single form _L_(_t_) = _P_ + _t_**v** with three ranges: all _t_ (line), _t_ ≥ 0 (ray), 0 ≤ _t_ ≤ 1 with **v** = _B_ − _A_ (segment). A hit is a number on the segment (G2). **Hook:** the turret in the maze fires a laser, a ray: how close does it pass to the ball? And two balls rolling toward each other: when are they closest?
- **The mathematics:** (1) The closest point on a line: _t_\* = (_Q_ − _P_)·**v**/(**v**·**v**), 1.5's projection ("el mismo movimiento"). (2) The distance two ways, with **u** = _Q_ − _P_: *d*² = |**u**|² − (**u**·**v**)²/|**v**|² (Pythagoras) and _d_ = |**u** × **v**|/|**v**| (area over base). L1 prefers the second: the first subtracts two large squares (2.9's cancellation). (3) Segment: clamp _t_ to [0, 1]; ray: clamp below only; compare against |**v**|² to defer the division (E). (4) Two points moving: **Δ** = *P*B − *P*A, **w** = **v**B − **v**A; minimize |**Δ** + **w**_t_|²: _t_\* = −**Δ**·**w**/|**w**|². **Correction:** MA writes it without the minus sign (its code too), and on its own exercise concludes "moving apart" for a head-on collision. (5) Two lines: the connecting segment is perpendicular to both, a 2×2 system with determinant −|**v**₁ × **v**₂|² (zero iff parallel). Two _segments_ need more than clamping: planted, solved in 3.7. **Worked examples:** segment _A_ = (0, 0, 0), _B_ = (4, 0, 0); _C_ = (1, 2, 0) gives _t_ = 0,25, foot (1, 0, 0), _d_ = 2; _C_ = (−2, 1, 0) gives _t_ = −0,5, clamped to _A_, _d_ = √5 ≈ 2,236. MA's own case: _A_ = (4, 2), **v**A = (3, 4), _B_ = (20, 12), **v**B = (−5, −1): **Δ** = (16, 10), **w** = (−8, −5), _t_\* = 178/89 = 2, both at (10, 10) (the book's sign gives −2).
- **The live figure:** a draggable segment and point (E Fig. 5.2); a selector line, ray or segment, colouring _t_ < 0, [0, 1] and > 1; the three regions shaded; both distance formulas live; two moving dots with MA's sign against the corrected one (the toggle). Perturbation: slide _C_ 1000 m along the line and switch to Float32: the Pythagoras formula collapses, the cross product holds. Panel: _t_, clamped _t_, foot, _d_ (Pythagoras), _d_ (cross), _t_\*. **Expected:** the numbers above; with _A_ = origin, **v** = (1, 0, 0), _C_ = (1000; 0,01; 0) in Float32: Pythagoras reads 0, the cross product 0,0100.
- **Traps:** a direction that is not unit makes _t_ a fraction, not metres (RTR); clamping both closest points of two segments is wrong (E Fig. 5.9), solved in 3.7; "parallel" means a determinant below a threshold (L1), never `=== 0`; not every API returns hits sorted by _t_ (G2).
- **Exercises:** (1) the distance from the ball to the turret's laser as a segment and as a ray (hint: the same _t_, a different clamp). (2) Derive _t_\* by completing the square, without calculus (hint: |**Δ** + **w**_t_|² = |**w**|²(_t_ − _t_\*)² + a constant). (3) "Corregí al autor": run MA's formula on its own exercise and explain the −2.
- **Our angle:** "el mismo movimiento" (projection, §6.2). Se reduce a: the closest approach of two movers is the closest point of a line to the origin, in relative coordinates — the seed of relative motion (3.15). Clamps are `Math.min`/`Math.max`, exact in every browser. Manifold's `castRay` takes a unit direction and a reach; 2-05 measured that with a direction of length 2 Rapier's `timeOfImpact` comes out in half metres.
- **Sources:** L1 3.3, Ex. 3.2, 3.3, 3.11; E §3.5, §5.1.2; RTR 22.2, 22.15; MA §3.3.14; G §5.6.1; G2 §14.3.7.1.
- **Reuses:** 2-05's Sutileza "la dirección tiene que medir 1", with its measured Rapier behaviour.
- **Open:** whether Plücker coordinates (L1 3.5) get a "Más a fondo" here or wait for part 10.

### 3.4 Planos (C)

- **Question:** ¿De qué lado de una pared está algo, y dónde la cruza un rayo?
- **Before:** 3.3; 1.10 (points with _w_ = 1, directions with _w_ = 0).
- **Leads:** L1 3.4 ([**n** | _d_] as a row, signed distance, reflection, line–plane, transforming planes). **Also:** L2 §6.4, §9.1 (regions as inward planes; the reflection matrix has det −1; clipping waits for 4.10); RTR 22.10 (plane–box by projected extent), 22.14.1 (kept for 8.14); E §3.6, §5.1.1, §5.3.1, §11.3.2 (halfspaces; convex region as their intersection; thick planes); MF §13.3.2 (plane against half-space); G §5.6.3.
- **Approach:** L1's implicit plane as a row of four numbers: every test is one 4D dot product with (_P_, 1), and the formulas' types make them right. Every query is "substitute the parametric form into the implicit one" (E), the move 3.9 repeats. Reflection goes in the main path (L1; §6.2). **Hook:** the floor and the maze's walls are planes. Is the ball in front of the wall? Where does the laser hit the floor? Which way does a ball bounce?
- **The mathematics:** (1) [**n** | _d_] with |**n**| = 1; signed distance _s_(_P_) = **n**·_P_ + _d_ ("how many unit normals fit between", L1); _k_[**n** | _d_] is the same plane. (2) Closest point _R_ = _P_ − _s_(_P_)**n**; reflection _P_′ = _P_ − 2*s*(_P_)**n**, the matrix **I** − 2**nn**ᵀ with translation −2*d* **n**, det −1: "el mismo movimiento" (split along **n**, flip that part). The bounce **v**′ = **v** − (1 + _e_)(**v**·**n**)**n**, only when **v**·**n** < 0, planted for 4.3. (3) Line–plane: _t_ = −(**n**·_O_ + _d_)/(**n**·**D**): a point above, a direction below (L1). (4) Halfspaces; a convex region is the intersection of inward halfspaces; a ground is a half-space, whose normal never flips (MF). (5) Plane–box by projected extent: _e_ = Σ *h*ᵢ|**n**·**u**ᵢ|; outside if _s_(_c_) − _e_ > 0, inside if _s_(_c_) + _e_ < 0, else straddling; only that diagonal matters because a linear function over the corners is largest when every term is positive (RTR). (6) A plane transforms as a row: [**n** | _d_]′ = [**n** | _d_]**H**⁻¹, needed even for a rigid **H** because _d_ changes. **Worked example:** floor [0, 1, 0 | 0]; a laser from _O_ = (0, 2, 0) along (0,6; −0,8; 0) hits at _t_ = 2,5, point (1,5; 0; 0). Wall _x_ = 3 facing −*x*: [−1, 0, 0 | 3]. A box at (2; 0,5; 0) with half 0,5: _s_ = 1, _e_ = 0,5, in front; turned 45° about _y_: _e_ = 0,707, still in front by 0,293. The ball at (4, 0, 1) m/s off that wall: **v**·**n** = −4, **v**′ = (−4, 0, 1).
- **The live figure:** a plane with its shaded half-space (lab kit); a draggable point with its signed distance as a bar and its foot _R_; a ray hitting the floor, greyed when _t_ < 0; a box classified by extent with _s_ and _e_ drawn; the reflected ghost of the ball. Toggle: plane against half-space for a ball that went past the wall (with the plane, the normal flips). Perturbations: a ray parallel to the floor; an origin on the floor. Panel: _s_, _R_, _t_, _e_, front / behind / straddling. **Expected:** the numbers above; parallel: _t_ shows ±Infinity and "no hit"; origin on the plane and parallel: NaN, and "no hit".
- **Traps:** reading distances before normalizing; transforming a plane like a point; the sign of _d_ differs between sources (E flips it); a computed hit is not exactly on the plane: classify with a **thick** plane, |_s_| ≤ ε (E §11.3.2), recalled in 3.16; the error grows as the ray grazes.
- **Exercises:** (1) translate a plane by **t**: _d_′ = _d_ − **n**·**t** (L1 Ex. 3.5). (2) Three planes meet at a point: derive it with 1.9's cross-product inverse (hint: the normals are the rows). (3) Prove that `t >= 0 && t <= 1` answers correctly when **n**·**D** = 0 (hint: how ±Infinity and NaN compare, E §11.2.2).
- **Our angle:** reflection is "el mismo movimiento" again; the plane–box extent is the seed of the separating axis (3.10) and of culling (8.14); polygon clipping waits for 4.10; 4.6's ground is a half-space. Se reduce a: one 4D dot product. Manifold's zones are first tested against the world-aligned box that holds the turned box (`holdingBox` in `world.ts`), this extent rule written as |**M**|**h**.
- **Sources:** L1 3.4; L2 §6.4, §9.1; RTR 22.10, 22.14.1; E §3.6, §5.1.1, §5.3.1, §11.2.2, §11.3.2; MF §13.3.2; G §5.6.3. Free: [Ericson's errata](https://realtimecollisiondetection.net/).
- **Reuses:** nothing.
- **Open:** Grassmann's join and meet (L1 4.2): a "Más a fondo" here or in part 10 (L1's reader suggests part 10).

### 3.5 Señalar con el mouse (P)

- **Question:** ¿Qué punto del piso está debajo del mouse?
- **Before:** 3.4 (ray–plane); 1.11 (the camera's world matrix); 1.2 and 1.3 (the pilot's `toScreen`).
- **Leads:** RTR 22.1 (picking on the CPU and on the GPU); L2 §6.1 (Eq. 6.6, the geometric route), §6.3.1; G2 §14.5.3.2 (render-query picking; the camera's ray is not the gun's). **Also:** G §10.2.1 (debug primitives with a lifetime).
- **Approach:** run 1.3's `toScreen` backwards, line by line (L2's geometric route): pixel → (_u_, _v_) in [−1, 1] → a point on the screen 1 m in front of the eye → a camera-space direction → a world direction by the camera's rotation (_w_ = 0, 1.10) → ray–plane with the floor. **Rejected:** unprojecting with the inverse projection matrix; our 1.10 matrix has no depth row and is singular (L2's trap), and its inversion waits for 8.3. **Hook:** the turret should turn to where you click. The first attempt puts the marker at (*p*ₓ, 0, *p*ᵧ) and it lands nowhere near the cursor.

### 3.6 Coordenadas baricéntricas (C) ◇

- **Question:** ¿Cómo se dice dónde está un punto dentro de un triángulo?
- **Before:** 3.4 (planes); 1.6 (signed areas, Lagrange's identity).
- **Leads:** E §3.4 (weights, ratios of signed areas, normalized heights; the seven regions; invariance under parallel projection), §5.4.2. **Also:** RTR 22.8 (the triangle written as (1 − _u_ − _v_)*p*₀ + _u_ *p*₁ + _v_ *p*₂, with sample values); L2 §5.5 (interpolation in rasterization, for part 8).
- **Approach:** three readings of the same three numbers (E): weights (a balance point), ratios of signed areas, heights over each edge. The main picture is the area ratio, because the reader owns signed area (1.6); the Cramer form is derived for the code. **Hook:** the maze's ramps are triangles: at what height is the floor under the ball? Is the click inside this triangle?
- **The mathematics:** (1) _P_ = _uA_ + _vB_ + _wC_, _u_ + _v_ + _w_ = 1; inside iff all three are ≥ 0. (2) _u_ = area(_PBC_)/area(_ABC_), signed by the normal. (3) Cramer: _P_ − _A_ = _v_**e**₀ + _w_**e**₁ with **e**₀ = _B_ − _A_, **e**₁ = _C_ − _A_; dotting with **e**₀ and **e**₁ gives a 2×2 system whose determinant is |**e**₀ × **e**₁|² (Lagrange's identity), zero iff the triangle is degenerate. (4) The seven sign regions. (5) Invariant under parallel projection: solve in the 2D shadow that drops the normal's largest component. (6) Interpolation _f_(_P_) = _u f\*(*A_) + _v f\*(*B_) + _w f\*(*C_): heights now, colours and texture coordinates in part 8. **Worked example:** on the floor (_x_, _z_): _A_ = (0, 0), _B_ = (4, 0), _C_ = (0, 4), _P_ = (1, 1): (_u_, _v_, _w_) = (0,5; 0,25; 0,25), area(_PBC_) = 4 of 8. Heights 0, 2, 1 at the corners: the floor is 0,75 m high at _P_. _P_ = (3, 3): _u_ = −0,5, outside edge _BC_.
- **The live figure:** drag _P_; _u_, _v_, _w_ as bars and as the three shaded sub-triangles; the seven regions coloured; a ramp in 3D with the ball resting at the interpolated height. Toggle: area ratios against Cramer (the same numbers: a brute-force check). Perturbation: squash the triangle toward a segment; the denominator goes to 0 and the guard trips. Panel: _u_, _v_, _w_, their sum, inside?, height. **Expected:** the values above; the sum 1,000 always.
- **Traps:** a degenerate triangle; barycentric regions are not Voronoi regions (E §3.10), the trap 3.7 shows; signs need a consistent winding (3.1).

### 3.7 El punto más cercano (C)

- **Question:** ¿Cuál es el punto de una forma más cerca de otro, y por qué casi todo se reduce a eso?
- **Before:** 3.3 (the clamp on a segment); 3.6 (regions); 1.11 (into an object's frame).
- **Leads:** E §5.1.1–5.1.5, §5.1.9, §3.10 (closest points on a plane, segment, box, turned box, triangle; two segments; Voronoi regions). **Also:** E §4.2.2 (test in one object's space).
- **Approach:** E's master query, one idea grown: **project, then clamp**. The closest point gives the distance, the contact normal (from the closest point to the centre), the depth, and a safe move ("moving less than the distance cannot collide"). **Hook:** the ball and a crate: touching? from which side? how deep? The naive test, "outside every face plane, so not touching", is false near an edge (E Fig. 5.18).
- **The mathematics:** (1) For a convex set _K_: _q_ is the closest point to _P_ iff (_P_ − _q_)·(_x_ − _q_) ≤ 0 for every _x_ in _K_; it is unique (parallelogram law: the midpoint of two minimizers is closer). (2) **Box:** |_P_ − _q_|² = Σ(*p*ᵢ − *q*ᵢ)² over a product of intervals, so each term is minimized on its own by clamping: _q_ = clamp(_P_, min, max). Face, edge and vertex regions are which coordinates got clamped. (3) **Turned box:** into its frame by three dot products (1.11), clamp, back. (4) **Triangle:** find the Voronoi region. Vertex _A_: (_P_ − _A_)·(_B_ − _A_) ≤ 0 and (_P_ − _A_)·(_C_ − _A_) ≤ 0. Edge _AB_: outside _AB_ and between the perpendicular planes at _A_ and _B_. Else the face: project on the plane. The cross-product version first, then E's reduction to six dot products through Lagrange's identity. (5) **Two segments:** clamping both line-closest points is wrong (E Fig. 5.9); clamp one, recompute the other, clamp again (E §5.1.9). (6) **Sphere against anything:** closest point, then compare |_P_ − _q_|² with *r*². **Worked examples:** box min (−1, 0, −1), max (1, 2, 1). _P_ = (3; 1; 0,5) → (1; 1; 0,5), face, _d_ = 2. _P_ = (3; 3; 0,5) → (1; 2; 0,5), edge, _d_ = √5 ≈ 2,236. _P_ = (3, 3, 2) → (1, 2, 1), vertex, _d_ = √6 ≈ 2,449. The obtuse trap: _A_ = (0, 0), _B_ = (4, 0), _C_ = (−2, 2), _P_ = (−2, −1): outside _AB_ and behind _A_, yet the closest point is (−0,5; 0,5) on _CA_, at 2,121 < |_PA_| = 2,236. Two segments (0, 0)–(1, 0) and (2, −1)–(4, 1): clamp-both gives 2,000, the truth is √2 ≈ 1,414.
- **The live figure:** (1) a box with a draggable _P_, the clamped _q_ and the lit region (E Fig. 5.3); (2) a triangle with its seven Voronoi regions, the barycentric regions overlaid (they differ), and the obtuse trap; (3) two segments, clamp-both against the true procedure (the toggle). A brute-force reference samples the shape densely and reports the largest disagreement. Panel: _q_, region, _d_, *d*², normal (_P_ − _q_)/_d_. **Expected:** the numbers above; brute-force disagreement below the sampling step.
- **Traps:** "outside every face, so disjoint" (false near edges); "outside _AB_ and behind _A_, so _A_" (false for obtuse triangles, E); Voronoi regions are not barycentric regions; clamping both segment points.
- **Exercises:** (1) the distance from a coin to a crate, and the ball's contact normal. (2) Prove that the closest point of a convex set is unique (hint: the parallelogram law). (3) The two segments above by hand.
- **Our angle:** E's signature, "se reduce a…": sphere–box, sphere–triangle and sphere–capsule (3.8), the walls (3.14), GJK (4.10), the character's capsule (5.1); the direction from _q_ to _P_ is the gradient of a distance function (10.2). Clamps are exact in every browser. Manifold's zones test a point in the zone's own frame, the same change of frame (`sense` in `world.ts`).
- **Sources:** E §5.1.1–5.1.5, §5.1.9, §3.10, §4.2.2. Free: Erin Catto, [_Computing Distance using GJK_](https://box2d.org/files/ErinCatto_GJK_GDC2010.pdf) (Voronoi regions of a simplex).
- **Reuses:** nothing.
- **Open:** keep E's six-dot-product triangle routine in the main path or in a "Más a fondo" (the cross-product version is the main path either way).

### 3.8 Volúmenes que envuelven (C)

- **Question:** ¿Cómo se pregunta rápido si dos cosas pueden estar tocándose?
- **Before:** 3.7; 1.11 (frames); 1.8 (the |**M**| rule, met as an exercise).
- **Leads:** E §4.1–4.3, §4.5 (desirable properties; AABBs, spheres, sphere-swept volumes; keeping a box around a turning object). **Also:** RTR 22.2–22.4, 22.13 (definitions, sphere builders, the fit figure, geometric probability), 19.1.1; L2 §9.3 (judge a volume by its surface area; 13 directions); G §5.6.2–5.6.7 (spheres as [_C_ _r_]; an OBB test by changing frame); MF §12.1–12.3 (a broad test must be conservative).
- **Approach:** a bounding volume never shows: it is a proxy that makes the common answer, "no", cheap. Each volume as E presents it: a representation, a test, a way to build it, a way to keep it under motion; and the trade-off from sphere to AABB to OBB to hull, cheap test against tight fit. **Hook:** 3.7 answers exactly, but testing the ball against every wall's exact shape each step is waste: most pairs are far apart. What is the cheapest test that never says "no" wrongly?
- **The mathematics:** (1) Conservative: false positives allowed, false negatives never (MF). (2) **AABB:** min–max, min–widths or centre–half; overlap iff the intervals overlap on every axis; centre form |*c*A − *c*B| ≤ *h*A + *h*B per axis. (3) **Spheres:** |*c*A − *c*B|² ≤ (*r*A + *r*B)², no square root. (4) **Capsule:** the points within _r_ of a segment; sphere–capsule is a point–segment distance against *r*₁ + *r*₂, capsule–capsule a segment–segment distance (3.7); Minkowski named here, proved in 3.11. (5) **A box around a turning box:** half-extents |**M**|**h** (entrywise absolute values); proof: the largest _x_ over the corners is Σⱼ|*m*ₓⱼ|*h*ⱼ, each term maximized by its corner sign. Never rebuild from last step's box: it grows by |cos| + |sin| per step. (6) **Spheres from points:** the AABB's centre, the vertex mean (up to twice too big), Ritter (grow to swallow each outside point: the centre moves (_d_ − _r_)/2, the radius becomes (_d_ + _r_)/2). **Worked examples:** a box of half (1; 0,5; 0,25) turned 30° about _y_: AABB half (0,991; 0,5; 0,717). A square of half 0,5 turning 10° per step with the rebuilt box: after 9 steps (90°) its half is 0,5 × 1,158⁹ ≈ 1,87, while the true one is 0,5 again.
- **The live figure:** the zoo on one draggable, turning shape (sphere, AABB, OBB, capsule, hull) with wasted area and test cost; a turning box with three boxes, tight, |**M**| and rebuilt-from-last-step (the toggle, the last one swelling); a 2D point cloud with the AABB-centre, vertex-mean and Ritter circles (Welzl in "Más a fondo"), radii listed; drag points into a cluster and watch the mean fail. Panel: radii, half extents, growth factor, false positives against the exact test. **Expected:** the numbers above; false negatives always 0.
- **Traps:** the vertex mean as a centre; rebuilding from last step's box; non-uniform scale turns a sphere into an ellipsoid (G2 §13.3.2.1); three.js computes `boundingSphere` and `boundingBox` once and centres its sphere on the box: stale after vertices move (L2's reader); Ritter's refinement and Welzl shuffle the points: feed them the seeded stream (2.10), or one level builds different volumes in two browsers (ours).
- **Exercises:** (1) derive the |**M**| rule in 2D (hint: the four corners). (2) Show that the vertex mean can be almost twice too big (hint: a long thin cloud with most points at one end). (3) Pick-up radii: a coin as a sphere against the ball, then against a capsule (the character of part 5).
- **Our angle:** Manifold's `holdingBox` is exactly the |**M**| rule: each zone is first tested against the world-aligned box that holds it, which made the step cheaper with the same hash (Bitácora 2.5b). "Más a fondo": Cauchy's theorem, the mean shadow of a convex body is a quarter of its surface (RTR 22.4), with a Monte Carlo figure on a seeded stream. Se reduce a: closest point and interval overlap.
- **Sources:** E §4.1–4.3, §4.5, §4.2.6; RTR 22.2–22.4, 22.13, 19.1.1; L2 §9.3; G §5.6.2–5.6.7; MF §12.1–12.3.
- **Reuses:** 1-01's Sutileza "la caja de algo girado es más grande que la cosa"; Manifold's `holdingBox`.

### 3.9 Rayos contra formas (C)

- **Question:** ¿Dónde choca un rayo con una esfera, una caja o un triángulo?
- **Before:** 3.6 (barycentric coordinates); 3.8 (AABBs, spheres); 3.4 (substituting a ray into an implicit equation).
- **Leads:** E §5.3, §5.4.2 (ray–sphere with early outs, slabs, ray–triangle by Cramer's rule); RTR 22.5–22.9 (each test twice; its rules of thumb; Möller–Trumbore as a shear into the unit triangle; the crossings test). **Also:** G2 §14.3.5.1–2 (squared lengths); Quilez (compact intersectors).
- **Approach:** one move (E): substitute the ray into the shape and solve for _t_; then redo it as a picture that rejects early (RTR): the same answer, cheaper failures. **Hook:** 3.5 picks the floor; now pick an object (the crate under the cursor), and make the turret's laser stop at the first thing it meets.
- **The mathematics:** (1) **Sphere, algebra:** with **m** = _O_ − _C_ and |**d**| = 1, *t*² + 2*bt* + _c_ = 0, _b_ = **m**·**d**, _c_ = **m**·**m** − *r*²; _t_ = −*b* ± √(*b*² − _c_); five cases (miss, tangent, two hits, origin inside, sphere behind). **Geometry:** _s_ = (_C_ − _O_)·**d** (projection), reject if _s_ < 0 with the origin outside; *m*² = |_C_ − _O_|² − *s*² (rejection), reject if *m*² > *r*²; _t_ = _s_ ∓ √(*r*² − *m*²). (2) **Box by slabs:** per axis *t*₁ = (minᵢ − *O*ᵢ)/*d*ᵢ, *t*₂ = (maxᵢ − *O*ᵢ)/*d*ᵢ, swapped if needed; entry = the largest entry, exit = the smallest exit; a hit iff entry ≤ exit and exit ≥ 0; a parallel axis: inside the slab or a miss. **Correction:** E's printed slab code updates the exit with the wrong comparison; the exit must keep the minimum (check the errata). (3) **Triangle:** _O_ + _t_**d** = (1 − _u_ − _v_)_A_ + _uB_ + _vC_, so [−**d**, **e**₁, **e**₂](_t_, _u_, _v_)ᵀ = _O_ − _A_; Cramer, each determinant a triple product (1.6); inside iff _u_, _v_ ≥ 0 and _u_ + _v_ ≤ 1; the determinant's sign tells front from back (3.1). (4) Many objects: the smallest positive _t_ wins, and a segment is a ray whose maximum _t_ shrinks with each hit (RTR). **Worked examples:** _O_ = (0, 1, 0), **d** = (1, 0, 0), sphere _C_ = (5; 1,5; 0), _r_ = 1: _b_ = −5, _c_ = 24,25, *b*² − _c_ = 0,75, _t_ = 4,134 and 5,866; geometrically _s_ = 5, *m*² = 0,25, √0,75 = 0,866. Slabs in 2D, box (2, −1)–(4, 1), _O_ = (0, 0): **d** = (0,8; 0,6) gives _x_ [2,5; 5], _y_ [−1,667; 1,667], entry 2,5 > exit 1,667, a miss; **d** = (0,96; 0,28) gives entry 2,083 ≤ exit 3,571, a hit at (2; 0,583). With the printed typo, the first ray reads exit 5 and reports a false hit. Triangle (0, 0, 0), (2, 0, 0), (0, 2, 0) and _O_ = (0,5; 0,5; 2), **d** = (0, 0, −1): _t_ = 2, (_u_, _v_) = (0,25; 0,25).

### 3.10 El eje que separa (C)

- **Question:** ¿Cómo se prueba que dos formas convexas no se tocan?
- **Before:** 3.8 (intervals on axes); 3.4 (the projected extent); 1.5 (the extreme point); 1.6 (cross products).
- **Leads:** E §5.2.1 (the test; least overlap), §4.4 (OBBs, their 15 axes, the robustness fix), §3.8. **Also:** RTR 22.12–22.13 ("test", not "theorem"; Fig. 22.6; the concave counterexample); L2 §9.4.2–9.4.3 (the effective radius; plane-by-plane culling is conservative, which motivates the edge axes); MF §13.4 (least overlap gives normal and depth); G2 §14.3.5.3–6 (a shadow on a wire); Gregorius (GDC 2013).
- **Approach:** a convex shape's shadow on a wire is an interval (G2); disjoint intervals on some axis prove separation. E's order: 2D first (face normals suffice), then 3D's edge × edge axes, then the axis of least overlap as normal and depth. It is the separating axis **test**, a corollary of the separating hyperplane theorem (RTR's correction of the old name). **Hook:** two turned crates in the maze; their AABBs overlap; 3.7 handles a point against a box, not a box against a box.
- **The mathematics:** (1) Separating hyperplane theorem for compact convex sets (statement; sketch: take the closest pair of points, 3.7, and the plane that bisects them). (2) A box's projected radius on **L**: _r_ = Σ *h*ᵢ|**u**ᵢ·**L**| (3.4's extent). (3) Separated on **L** iff |**T**·**L**| > *r*A + *r*B, with **T** the centres' difference. (4) Which axes: contacts are face–face, face–edge or edge–edge; when two edges are the closest features, the segment between their closest points is perpendicular to both, so along their cross product (E); RTR's squeeze argument. Boxes: 3 + 3 + 9 = 15 axes; in 2D, 2 + 2. (5) Least overlap _o_ = *r*A + *r*B − |**T**·**L**| (unit **L**) gives the contact normal and an estimate of the depth, the shortest separating translation (E, MF). (6) Robustness: parallel edges give **L** = 0, every projection is 0, the test reads "0 > 0" and float noise can "separate" overlapping boxes; add ε to the radii (E §4.4.2) or skip near-zero axes. **Worked example (2D):** _A_ at the origin, half (1, 1), aligned; _B_ half (1, 1) turned 45°. At (2,5; 0,5): on _A_'s _x_, 2,5 > 1 + 1,414, separated. At (2,3; 0,5): every axis overlaps (by 0,114 on _x_, 1,914 on _y_, 0,434 and 1,141 on _B_'s axes): touching, normal along _x_, depth 0,114.
- **The live figure:** 2D: two draggable, turnable polygons; every candidate axis drawn with both shadows as intervals (lab kit); the first separating axis lit; "overlap mode" draws the least-overlap axis as the normal with its depth. 3D: two crossed boxes almost edge to edge, where the six face axes say "overlap" and one edge × edge axis separates; the naive "face axes only" reports a false contact. The concave counterexample (a bucket in a well). Perturbation: make two edges parallel and switch to Float32; without ε a spurious separation appears. Panel: per axis |**T**·**L**|, *r*A, *r*B, overlap; separated?; normal; depth. **Expected:** the numbers above.
- **Traps:** convex shapes only; parallel edges (the zero axis); comparisons work with unnormalized axes, depths do not; the axis count grows as *E*A·*E*B, which is why engines want simple shapes. **Correction:** E's printed OBB test has an index typo in one component of the translation (check the errata); test it against a brute-force reference (dense sampling).

### 3.11 Convexidad y la suma de Minkowski (C) ◇

- **Question:** ¿Por qué agrandar una forma y achicar la otra no cambia la pregunta?
- **Before:** 3.7 (closest point); 3.10 (convexity, the separating axis).
- **Leads:** E §3.8, §3.11, ch. 9 intro, §4.5 (convex sets, support points, the Minkowski sum and difference, sphere-swept volumes). **Also:** RTR 22.14 (a volume against a region is a point against the grown region, Fig. 22.25); G2 §14.3.5.5–6 (one algorithm for all convex pairs through support functions).
- **Approach:** E's three facts about convex sets (a separating plane exists; a local minimum of distance is global; the support point describes the shape), then the Minkowski sum and difference, which turn shape against shape into point against shape. **Hook:** 3.8 said "a capsule is a segment plus a radius" without proof; 3.14 needs the ball against a box as its centre against a rounded box; 3.15 needs a moving ball as a ray. One idea instead of three tricks.
- **The mathematics:** (1) _A_ ⊕ _B_ = {_a_ + _b_}, _A_ ⊖ _B_ = _A_ ⊕ (−*B*). (2) _A_ and _B_ overlap iff 0 ∈ _A_ ⊖ _B_; dist(_A_, _B_) = min over _A_ ⊖ _B_ of |_c_| (one line each, from the definitions). (3) The support point *s*K(**d**), the extreme point along **d** (1.5); *s*A⊖B(**d**) = *s*A(**d**) − *s*B(−**d**), because the maximum of a linear function over a difference splits. Supports: a sphere _c_ + _r_**d**/|**d**|; a box _c_ + Σ sign(**u**ᵢ·**d**)*h*ᵢ**u**ᵢ; a capsule, its segment's support plus _r_**d**/|**d**|. (4) The sum of two convex polygons is convex; its edges are both polygons' edges merged by angle. (5) For convex shapes a local minimum of distance is global (E Fig. 9.1): greedy search works, which is why engines want convex pieces. (6) A ball of radius _r_ against a box is its centre against the box ⊕ ball, a rounded box. **Worked example:** the square [0, 2]² ⊕ the triangle (0, 0), (1, 0), (0, 1) is the pentagon (0, 0), (3, 0), (3, 2), (2, 3), (0, 3).
- **The live figure:** two draggable convex polygons; _A_ ⊖ _B_ drawn live with the origin marked; the origin is inside iff they overlap; the shortest vector from the origin to _A_ ⊖ _B_ equals the shortest vector between _A_ and _B_ (E Figs. 3.27, 9.10). Then a disc dragged around a box, its centre tracing the rounded box (RTR Fig. 22.25). Panel: origin inside?, distance to the origin, distance between the shapes by brute force, support values. **Expected:** the pentagon's vertices; the two distances equal to three decimals.
- **Traps:** rotation breaks it: a turning shape swept along a segment is not a Minkowski sum (G2: sweeps are exact only for translation); concave shapes have local minima that are not global; ⊖ does not undo ⊕ ((_A_ ⊕ _B_) ⊖ _B_ ≠ _A_ in general, an aside for the mathematician).
- **Exercises:** (1) the sum of a square and a triangle by hand (E Fig. 3.26). (2) Prove *s*A⊖B(**d**) = *s*A(**d**) − *s*B(−**d**). (3) Write the supports of a box and of a capsule.
- **Our angle:** configuration space, where the obstacle grows and the robot becomes a point (2-05's "el mundo engordado"); 4.10's GJK is this lesson plus 3.7's regions; the shape casts of part 5 are this. For the author, the most beautiful object in E. Se reduce a: a point against a grown shape.
- **Sources:** E §3.8, §3.11, §4.5, ch. 9 intro; RTR 22.14; G2 §14.3.5.5–6. Free: [Catto, GJK (GDC 2010)](https://box2d.org/files/ErinCatto_GJK_GDC2010.pdf).
- **Reuses:** 2-05's "Más a fondo" "Barrer una pelota es tirar un rayo contra el mundo engordado": the slot, 4,75 m against 5 m, and the corner equation (2,9 − _x_)² + 0,2² = 0,25², giving _x_ = 2,75.
- **Open:** if folded, it becomes the last section of 3.10, keeping the Minkowski figure.

### 3.12 Muchos objetos: partir el espacio (C)

- **Question:** ¿Cómo se evita probar cada par entre cientos de cosas?
- **Before:** 3.8 (AABB overlap); 2.4 (things move every step); 2.10 (a seeded crowd).
- **Leads:** E ch. 7 (grids and their cell size, hashed grids, sort and sweep, time stamps), ch. 6 (BVHs); GPP ch. 20 (the 1D framing, the design space, the subtleties). **Also:** RTR 19.1 (four shapes of one idea; scene graphs are not spatial structures); MF §12.4 (multiresolution maps); Catto (2019: dynamic AABB trees, the surface-area heuristic).
- **Approach:** GPP's framing. The question is "what is near here?", asked by physics, sound and minds alike. Simplify to one dimension (sort, then search) and generalize: a grid is a bucket sort that persists; BVHs and k-d trees are binary search trees; quadtrees are tries. E gives the numbers and the traps. One lesson, with the BVH as a section (curriculum). The figure is a crowd of hundreds of balls, since the maze has too few objects to show the *n*² wall. **Hook:** a crowd of 2000 balls tested all against all: two million tests every step.
- **The mathematics:** (1) _n_(_n_ − 1)/2 pairs. (2) A grid of side _s_ over an area _A_ with _n_ objects spread evenly: about *n*²*s*²/(2*A*) tests plus the neighbour cells; all in one cell, back to *n*²: an expected gain, not a guarantee (GPP). (3) The cell-size rule: _s_ at least the largest object at any rotation, so each object touches at most 4 cells in 2D and 8 in 3D; too small, many cells per object; too large, all against all; mixed sizes, both (E Fig. 7.1). (4) Each pair once: half the neighbour stencil (provable), or a time stamp per object (E §7.7.2). (5) A hashed grid: a `Map` keyed by an integer cell key gives an infinite world with memory proportional to the objects (E §7.1.3). (6) Sort and sweep: sorted interval ends on one axis; insertion sort on an almost-sorted list is nearly linear (coherence); objects resting on a floor cluster on _y_, so sweep on _x_ or _z_. (7) BVH: built top-down by splitting at the median of the centroids; a query descends only into children it touches. **Worked numbers** (1-01's, to re-measure with the seeded crowd): 300 balls, 44 850 pairs against about 1 400 grid tests; 2000 balls, about 2 million against about 60 000; cells of 0,25, 0,5 and 4 m, about 55 000, 40 000 and 190 000 tests.
- **The live figure:** _n_ balls (up to 2000) moving in a box; modes all-pairs (a grey fog of lines at _n_ = 200), grid (cell-size slider, occupancy, the 8- against 4-neighbour stencil with double counts highlighted), sort and sweep (ends on _x_, swaps per step, a "settle on the floor" button that shows _y_ clustering), and a BVH over static boxes with a ray descending it. A "cluster" button herds everyone into one cell. The brute-force reference compares every step's pairs with all-pairs: "same pairs: yes" (E §2.7.1). One knob, two failures: the cell size, with the test count measured at both ends. Panel: _n_, pairs, tests per step, pairs found, same pairs?, nodes visited. **Expected:** "same pairs: yes" always; the counts above within 15 %.
- **Traps:** forgetting the neighbour cells (pairs missed at borders); checking all eight (double hits); a broad phase that misses pairs is a silent bug, hence the reference; moving an object between cells; with few objects the structure costs more than it saves (1-01 measured this at 300 balls). Determinism: the pair order depends on insertion order; fix it (insert in index order, or sort the pairs), because a resolver that walks pairs in order gives another result in another order (1-01's Sutileza).
- **Exercises:** (1) count the tests for _n_ = 10, 100, 1000, all against all. (2) Prove that half the stencil counts each pair exactly once. (3) Add sort and sweep to 1-01's broad phase and compare its tests with the grid's.
- **Our angle:** the same structure answers rays (3.17's grid walk), hearing (7.10) and culling (8.14). Rapier's broad phase is a tree that also answers queries and is brought up to date once per step, so a collider created between two steps is invisible to queries until the next one (measured in 2-05). On a world glued to itself, the hashed grid's indices simply wrap (10.7). Se reduce a: sorting.
- **Sources:** E ch. 6–7; GPP ch. 20; RTR 19.1; MF §12.4. Free: Catto, [_Dynamic Bounding Volume Hierarchies_](https://box2d.org/files/ErinCatto_DynamicBVH_GDC2019.pdf) (GDC 2019); Matthias Müller, [_Ten Minute Physics_](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/tenMinutePhysics/index.html) 11, 23, 24; Nystrom, [_Spatial Partition_](https://gameprogrammingpatterns.com/spatial-partition.html).
- **Reuses:** 1-01 nearly whole: its example `stage-1/01-finding-collisions` (`overlap`, `everyPair`, `grid`, the instanced crowd, the "same pairs as every pair" check), its numbers and its exercises (sort and sweep; a tiny solver, which moves to 4.3).

### 3.13 El presupuesto del cuadro (C) ◇

- **Question:** ¿Cuánto tarda cada cosa, y cómo se mide antes de optimizar?
- **Before:** 3.12 (the first algorithm whose cost the reader can measure); 2.4 (the frame and the step).
- **Leads:** G §2.3 (profilers; most of the time is in a little of the code), §10.8 (hierarchical in-game profiling); RTR 18.2 (find the bottleneck by changing one part's work). **Also:** G §8.5.1 (the budget); E §2.5 (the worst case matters more than the average); the browsers' profilers.
- **Approach:** G's engine-builder rule: measure first. A frame is 16,7 ms, and the worst frames are what players feel (E). RTR's experiment finds the bottleneck: change one part's work and watch the total. **Hook:** in 3.12 the grid looked faster, and at 300 balls it was not; intuition about cost misleads, so the project gets a frame bar.
- **The mathematics:** light and exact. The budget: a 4 ms system is 24 % of a 60 Hz frame. Percentiles: the 95th, not the mean. A cost model _T_(_n_) = _a_ + _bn_ + *cn*² fitted from three measurements tells which term dominates. Amdahl's bound: speeding up a part that takes a fraction _p_ of the frame by _k_ gives 1/((1 − _p_) + _p_/_k_). **Worked example:** collision at 30 % made 10 times faster speeds the frame up 1/(0,7 + 0,03) ≈ 1,37 times. And Manifold's own: a physics step took 0,47–0,65 ms for 40–200 cubes, and the hash, first 5,5 ms at 200 cubes (more than the physics), was 1,8 ms after a fix (Bitácora 1a): the measurement found the surprise.
- **The live figure:** 3.12's crowd with a stacked frame bar (broad phase, narrow phase, drawing, other) and a histogram of frame times with the mean and the 95th percentile. Perturbations: an allocation burst (a garbage-collection spike), a frame spike, a hidden tab. RTR's experiment: double one part's work and see whether the total moves. Panel: ms per section (mean, 95th), frame total, budget used. **Expected:** times depend on the machine, so the browser test reads relations, not values: the sections add up to at most the total; the 95th percentile is at least the mean; doubling the broad phase's work raises its bar.
- **Traps:** `performance.now()` is coarse in browsers (average over many steps), and it is a clock read: it lives in the view, never in the state (§4.5, rule 5). Warm-up: the first steps include compilation (Manifold's first Rapier step takes about 100 ms). Garbage-collection pauses show as spikes: capsule _Rendimiento en el navegador_, with reused temporaries only where the profiler asks (decision 0004). Measure on the slowest target, an integrated GPU.

(2) Find the cell size that minimizes the broad phase's time rather than its test count: are they the same? (3) Record a profile in Chrome or Firefox and name the three costliest functions.

### 3.14 Chocar con las paredes, y deslizarse (P)

- **Question:** ¿Cómo se queda la pelota fuera de las paredes, y se desliza en vez de frenar?
- **Before:** 3.7 (the closest point on a box); 3.8 (AABBs); 3.12 (the grid); 2.8 (the ball moved by actions, semi-implicit Euler, the floor as a clamp); 3.13 (the frame bar).
- **Leads:** E §5.2.5–5.2.7 (sphere against AABB, OBB, triangle by the closest point), §2.4.2 (sequential motion); RTR 22.13.2, 22.14; G2 §14.3.1, §14.3.7.2, §14.3.8, §14.5.3.6 (collision shapes; casts; filtering and materials; character mechanics); Fauerby (collide and slide). **Also:** MF §7.1.3, §7.2.2 (this is a contact with _e_ = 0 and no friction); L1 1.6, 3.4.6.
- **Approach:** detection by the closest point (E), response by "el mismo movimiento" (L1): push out along the normal by the depth, then remove the velocity's inward part. Named a contact with _e_ = 0 and no friction (MF), recalled in 4.3. **Hook:** the ball of 2.8 rolls through the walls. The naive fix, "if inside a wall, go back to where it was", stops it dead against any wall it brushes and traps it in corners.
- **The mathematics:** the contact from 3.7: _q_ = closestPointOnBox(_c_, box), **δ** = _c_ − _q_, _d_ = |**δ**|; a contact iff _d_ < _r_, with **n** = **δ**/_d_ and depth _r_ − _d_ (a centre inside the box has _d_ = 0: take the face of least penetration). Push out: _c_ ← _c_ + depth·**n**. Slide: if **v**·**n** < 0, **v** ← **v** − (**v**·**n**)**n** (only the inward part, or the ball sticks to walls it leaves, L1). Several walls: a fixed number of passes, walls in index order, the deepest first; in a corner the slide ends along the crease **n**₁ × **n**₂ (L1 3.4.6). Sequential motion (E §2.4.2): move, then collide, so "nothing overlaps" holds at the end of every step. **Worked example:** _r_ = 0,25, _c_ = (1,1; 0,25; 0,5), **v** = (−3, 0, 2), a wall from (0, 0, −2) to (1, 2, 2): _q_ = (1; 0,25; 0,5), _d_ = 0,1, **n** = (1, 0, 0), depth 0,15; _c_ ← (1,25; 0,25; 0,5); **v**·**n** = −3, **v** ← (0, 0, 2): it slides along the wall at 2 m/s.
- **The project:** `collide.ts` (new): `Box`, `Contact`, `closestPointOnBox(p, box)`, `sphereBox(center, radius, box): Contact | null`, `pushOut(center, contact)`, `slide(velocity, normal)`, `collideBall(ball, walls, grid)`. `grid.ts` (new): `Grid` (hashed, integer cell keys), `insert(id, box)`, `near(box)` (ids in index order). Walls get `group` (the ball meets `WALL`, not `TRIGGER`) and `material` (an id, coloured in debug; its numbers arrive in 4.5–4.6). `profile.ts` (new, view only): `mark(section)`, the frame bar. `debug.ts` draws contacts (point, normal, depth). The ball's step: actions → velocity (2.8) → integrate → `collideBall`. Playable: a room of walls and pillars (the maze arrives in 3.17); the ball slides along walls and into corners. Console: `show.contacts on` and `show.frame on` (view), `ball.speed 12` (world), `collide.passes 1` (world). **Recipe:** `seed 7; restart; step 600; hash` gives the lesson's recorded value in every browser; `collide.passes 1; restart; step 600; hash` gives another (the autopilot's corner now ends elsewhere).
- **Traps:** **a seam snag:** a wall made of two adjacent boxes; near their shared edge the ball's centre lies in the second box's edge region, so if that box is processed first its contact normal is diagonal and the ball stops at the seam (a ghost collision). Fixed where it lives: adjacent walls are merged into one box (3.17's maze emits runs) and contacts are processed deepest first. "Outside every face plane, so no contact" is false near an edge (E Fig. 5.18). A fast ball tunnels (`ball.speed 40`: 3.15's problem). Capsule _Del modelo al motor_: collision shapes are boxes, never the drawn wall meshes (G2 §14.3.1). Determinism: passes in a fixed order, walls in index order.
- **Exercises:** (1) the ball in a corner between two walls: predict where it ends after one step (hint: two push-outs; the slide is along **n**₁ × **n**₂). (2) Give walls a bounce that reflects instead of sliding (3.4's **v** − (1 + _e_)(**v**·**n**)**n** with _e_ = 1), and notice that it is 4.3 already. (3) A `TRIGGER` box the ball passes through that logs "entered".
- **Our angle:** "el mismo movimiento" (rejection). Manifold's characters slide through Rapier's character controller with a small gap (`CHARACTER_GAP = 0.02` in `physics.ts`), and the engine measured ghost collisions at full size: a ball rolling over adjacent boxes jumps at the seams, and Rapier's `FIX_INTERNAL_EDGES` is not used (Bitácora, the tutorial's stage). Collision groups are 16 bits of membership and 16 of filter (2-05). Fauerby's recursive collide-and-slide is what character controllers descend from (5.1).
- **Sources:** E §5.2.5–5.2.7, §2.4.2; RTR 22.13.2, 22.14; G2 §14.3.1, §14.3.7.2, §14.3.8, §14.5.3.6; MF §7.1.3, §7.2.2; L1 1.6, 3.4.6. Free: Kasper Fauerby, [_Improved Collision Detection and Response_](https://www.peroxide.dk/papers/collision/collision.pdf).
- **Reuses:** 2-05's collision groups (`groups`, 16 + 16 bits); Manifold's measured findings.

### 3.15 Lo que va rápido atraviesa (C)

- **Question:** ¿Cómo se sabe si algo chocó entre un paso y el siguiente?
- **Before:** 3.9 (ray–sphere, slabs); 3.11 (Minkowski); 3.14 (the walls the fast ball crosses).
- **Leads:** E §5.5 (dynamic tests: sampling, interval halving, the separating axis in time, a moving sphere against a plane, a sphere and a box), §2.4.3 (discrete against continuous motion). **Also:** G2 §14.3.5.7 (tunnelling, sweeps, continuous detection; rotation breaks swept convexity); Catto (2013: time of impact, conservative advancement).
- **Approach:** E over G2: the walls lesson works with a slow ball, and speeding it up is the part's best "El problema". Two moves turn the new problem into solved ones: relative motion (subtract one velocity, so one object stands still) and Minkowski (shrink the mover to a point, grow the other). **Hook:** `ball.speed 40` in 3.14's room: the ball shows on both sides of a 10 cm wall and never inside it.
- **The mathematics:** (1) Tunnelling: a ball of radius _r_ at speed _v_ with step _h_ can skip a wall of thickness _w_ when _vh_ > 2*r* + _w_. (2) A moving sphere against a sphere: **s** = *C*₀ − *C*₁, **v** = **v**₀ − **v**₁, _r_ = *r*₀ + *r*₁; |**s** + _t_**v**|² = *r*² gives (**v**·**v**)*t*² + 2(**v**·**s**)_t_ + (**s**·**s** − *r*²) = 0, and its first root in [0, _h_] is the time of impact: literally 3.9's ray against a sphere of radius *r*₀ + *r*₁ (E Fig. 5.35). Test overlap at _t_ = 0 first. (3) A moving sphere against a box: the centre's segment against the box grown by _r_ (3.11's rounded box): slabs on the box grown by _r_; a hit in an edge or corner region is redone against that edge's capsule or that corner's sphere (E §5.5.7). (4) The separating axis in time: on each axis, the interval of times when the shadows overlap; first contact is the largest start, if it is at most the smallest end: slabs in time are slabs in space (E's unspoken parallel, 3.9). (5) Interval halving: bound each half-motion by a sphere and recurse where it touches, with a depth limit. **Worked examples:** _r_ = 0,25, _v_ = 40 m/s, _h_ = 1/60: 0,667 m per step against 2*r* + _w_ = 0,6 for _w_ = 0,1: it tunnels. Two coins of _r_ = 0,1 at _x_ = 0 and _x_ = 2, moving at +3 and −1 m/s: impact at _t_ = 0,45 s (a gap of 1,8 m closing at 4 m/s).
- **The live figure:** a speed slider and a thin wall: the discrete samples, the sampled motion with its gaps (E Fig. 5.32), and the swept capsule with its time of impact (lab kit); discrete against swept (the toggle). The moving-sphere pair recast as a ray against the grown sphere, step by step. The separating axis in time: two shadows sliding along an axis, their contact interval on a time bar. Perturbation: a **step slider** (_h_ from 1/240 to 1/15 s), not a frame rate: with a fixed step, frames per second do not change what the simulation sees. Panel: _vh_, 2*r* + _w_, tunnels?, time of impact, contact point. **Expected:** the numbers above; at _h_ = 1/15 s the discrete test tunnels from (2*r* + _w_)/_h_ = 9 m/s.
- **Traps:** test overlap at _t_ = 0 (a small sphere inside a big one never "comes into contact", E); interval halving can recurse without end when moving parallel just outside the threshold (E); rotation during the step is ignored (piecewise-linear motion, E, G2). **Correction:** a box-corner helper in E's printed code for these tests has a typo (check the errata for its location); test the routine against fine sampling.
- **Exercises:** (1) the thinnest wall the ball's dash cannot cross (hint: _w_ > _vh_ − 2*r*). (2) The coins' time of impact by hand. (3) Write interval halving with a depth limit and compare its time of impact with the exact one.
- **Our angle:** se reduce a: a ray against a grown sphere. Continuous detection returns in 4.12 (Rapier's per-body switch), shape casts in 5.1 and the camera's sphere cast in 5.5; relative motion again in 5.8 (platforms). Rapier's `castShape` answers this query (2-05). The time of impact uses + − × ÷ √ only, the same in every browser.
- **Sources:** E §5.5, §2.4.3; G2 §14.3.5.7. Free: Catto, [_Continuous Collision_](https://box2d.org/files/ErinCatto_ContinuousCollision_GDC2013.pdf) (GDC 2013); [Rapier's CCD page](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/javascript/rigid_body_ccd).
- **Reuses:** 2-05's swept ball (the ray stops at 5 m, the ball at 4,75); 1-01's Sutileza on tunnelling.
- **Open:** locate the corner-helper typo in E's errata before quoting it.

### 3.16 Que nada atraviese el piso: geometría robusta (C) ◇

- **Question:** ¿Por qué una pelota se cuela entre dos triángulos, y cómo se diseña para que no pase?
- **Before:** 2.9 (floats, tolerances, non-associativity); 3.9 (ray–triangle); 3.14 (the seam snag); 3.1 (welding, T-junctions).
- **Leads:** E §11.3.2–11.5 (thick planes, shared calculations, fat objects, interval arithmetic, exact predicates), §12.1–12.3 (welding, cracks), §2.6. **Also:** L1 3.3.2–3.3.3 (formulas chosen for precision); RTR 22.8 (watertight ray–triangle tests).
- **Approach:** E's thesis, which is the author's own rule: robustness is designed in, not patched. Two sources: numbers that are not exact (2.9) and geometry that is not well formed (3.1). **Hook:** a ball rolling over a floor of two triangles drops through their shared edge; two boxes "separate" while touching (3.10's zero axis); the ball snags on a seam (3.14).
- **The mathematics:** (1) A predicate is wrong exactly when its value is near zero (E §11.1). (2) A thick plane: front, on or behind with |_s_| ≤ ε as "on"; ε from the scale and the operation count: a float32 near 3 m resolves about 0,24 µm, near 100 m about 7,6 µm (2⁻¹⁷); a float64 near 100 m, about 1,4 · 10⁻¹⁴ m. (3) Shared calculation: both triangles' inside tests use the same triple product for the shared edge, with its vertices ordered by index; then a ray across the edge hits at least one (proof: the two signs are the same number, negated). (4) Fat objects: test a sphere of radius ρ instead of a point; a crack narrower than 2ρ cannot be slipped through. (5) Equality with a tolerance is not transitive (2.9): never sort or hash with it. (6) Exact predicates: integers below 2⁵³ add and multiply exactly in doubles while every result stays below 2⁵³, so an orientation test on integer grid coordinates is exact: the bridge to determinism (E §11.5). (7) Welding with a tolerance, checked by 3.1's counts.
- **The live figure:** two floor triangles sharing an edge, and a ray sweeping across the edge in tiny steps, with a "missed both" counter for the naive test (each triangle its own plane hit and edge tests) and for the shared one, in Float32 (`Math.fround`); the thick plane (E Fig. 11.6); a capsule over a crack (E Fig. 11.10); a T-junction and its welding; 3.14's seam snag with separate and merged boxes. The naive-against-correct toggle is the whole figure. Panel: rays tried, missed both (naive), missed both (shared), ε. **Expected:** shared: 0, always; naive: more than 0 in Float32 (to be confirmed when the example is built).
- **Traps:** ordering the shared edge _AB_ in one triangle and _BA_ in the other undoes the fix (E); widening tolerances turns near misses into hits, the safe direction for collision (E); an assert such as "the hit point lies on the plane" needs its own tolerance (E). "Más a fondo": interval arithmetic proves that two things do not touch.
- **Exercises:** (1) find a ray that slips through with the naive test in Float32, then fix it. (2) Choose ε for a thick plane in a 100 m world in float32 (hint: the gap between floats there is 2⁻¹⁷ m). (3) Prove the shared-edge argument.
- **Our angle:** "the same expression, in the same order, gives the same answer" is at once E's robustness rule and our determinism rule (§6.1, shared predicates). Manifold's measured findings: Rapier computes in 32 bits, so a billionth of a metre vanishes near 3 m (Bitácora 1a), and adjacent boxes produce ghost collisions at their seams (Rapier's `FIX_INTERNAL_EDGES`, unused). The maze merges its walls (3.17); fat rays return as the camera's sphere cast (5.5) and the guard's sight (7.9).

### 3.17 El juego: el laberinto (P)

- **Question:** ¿Cómo se juega un laberinto distinto para cada semilla, y siempre con salida?
- **Before:** 3.13 (the frame bar); 3.15 (the swept test); 2.10 (seeded streams); also 3.14 (`collide.ts`), 3.5 (picking), 3.9 (rays), 2.13 (phases, coins, the turret as antagonist).
- **Leads:** MA §8.4.1–8.4.2 (depth-first backtracking; Prim's algorithm); E §7.4.2 (walking a ray through a grid); Buck (maze algorithms). **Also:** GPP ch. 7, ch. 4 (states and events, from 2.12–2.13).
- **Approach:** a maze is a spanning tree of the grid graph (MA): between any two cells there is exactly one path, and a few extra passages add loops that make chases interesting (part 7). The level's grid is also its broad phase and its sight structure (E). **Hook:** the game asks for a new maze per seed that can always be solved; "always" is a theorem, not a hope.
- **The mathematics:** (1) Depth-first backtracking: from the start, go to a random unvisited neighbour (the `maze` stream), pushing; at a dead end, pop. It visits every cell (connected) and only joins a new cell to the visited set (no cycle), so it builds a spanning tree with _mn_ − 1 passages and unique paths (induction on the visited set). (2) _k_ loops drawn from the stream: _mn_ − 1 + _k_ passages, still connected. (3) Walls as boxes: maximal runs of wall segments emitted as single boxes (fewer seams, 3.16). (4) The grid walk (Amanatides and Woo): keep the _t_ of the next vertical and horizontal boundary, step across whichever is nearer; it visits every pierced cell, which a Bresenham line does not (it can skip a corner cell, E Fig. 7.16). (5) The dash: the ball's centre swept against the near walls' rounded boxes (3.15), stopping at the time of impact. **Worked example:** a 12 × 12 maze has 143 passages; with _k_ = 6 loops, 149; with 2 m cells it is 24 m across.
- **The project:** `maze.ts` (new): `generateMaze(rng, cols, rows): Maze`, `addLoops(maze, rng, k)`, `wallsOf(maze): Box[]` (merged runs), `walkGrid(maze, ray, reach): Hit | null`. `collide.ts` grows `sweepBallBox(center, radius, motion, box): number | null`. `pick.ts`: a right click drops a breadcrumb on the floor point; clicking a turret toggles its view cone (ray against the turrets' boxes, 3.9: the object picking 3.9 prepared). `game.ts` gains the mode `maze`: phases ready, playing, won, lost (2.13's state machine); events `coinTaken`, `seen`, `exitReached`. The exit is the farthest cell by path length (a breadth-first search over the tree, planting 7.2). Turrets see the ball by cone and `walkGrid`. Console: `maze.seed 42` (startup), `show.colliders on` (view: the wall boxes, not the meshes), `show.frame on` (view), `maze.stats` (prints the counts). **Recipe:** `maze.seed 42; restart; maze.stats` prints `cells 144, passages 149, wall boxes N` (N recorded by the lesson's test); `seed 7; maze.seed 42; restart; step 3600; hash` gives the recorded value in every browser.
- **Traps:** generate from the maze's own stream (2.10), never from the coins' or the turrets'; the recursive version overflows the stack on big mazes: use an explicit stack; an exit placed by straight-line distance can be one wall away from the start, so use path length; Bresenham sight lets turrets see through corners. Determinism: the neighbours are listed in a fixed order (N, E, S, W) before the random choice.
- **Exercises:** (1) prove that the backtracker's maze has exactly one path between any two cells. (2) Add Prim's algorithm (MA §8.4.2) and compare the look (long corridors against many short dead ends). (3) A turret that sees through a crack: make its sight a fat ray (3.16) and watch the leak disappear.
- **Our angle:** 7.2's first graph is this tree, and the guard of part 7 will chase in it; the frame bar stays under budget. Manifold measured why a generator needs a fixed hash per seed ("nada fija el calabozo": the same hash compared across browsers cannot catch an unintended change to the generator itself), so the lesson's test records `maze.stats` and a hash per seed.
- **Sources:** MA §8.4.1–8.4.2; E §7.4.2, Fig. 7.16; GPP ch. 4, 7. Free: Jamis Buck, [_Maze Generation: Algorithm Recap_](https://weblog.jamisbuck.org/2011/2/7/maze-generation-algorithm-recap); [Red Blob Games](https://www.redblobgames.com/) (grids).
- **Reuses:** nothing from the archive directly (3-10's dungeon goes to 6.14); Manifold's finding on fixing a generator by a recorded hash per seed.
- **Open:** the grid walk is taught inside this project lesson, with no concept lesson before it (see the problems); the number of loops _k_ and the coin count are tuning, chosen when the game is played.

## Problems found in the curriculum

Each sheet above keeps the curriculum as approved; these are the problems found while designing it.

1. **3.5's "inverse of the projection" cannot be a matrix inverse.** The projection matrix of 1.10 has no depth row (it waits for 8.3), so it is singular, as L2's reader warned. 3.5 must invert 1.3's `toScreen` geometrically (pixel → direction → world ray). The sheet does so.
2. **3.9 is a concept lesson, but the curriculum says "picking objects closes 3.9".** A concept lesson does not change the project (0021). The 3.9 figure picks objects in its own scene; object picking enters the project in 3.17 (clicking a turret).
3. **3.14 gives walls "a material" before materials mean anything.** Restitution and friction arrive in 4.5–4.6. Until then the material is an id (drawn in debug); 4.6 extends it with numbers under the same name.
4. **3.14 uses 3.13's frame bar but its "Antes" column omits 3.13.** Harmless in a linear reading, and if 3.13 folds into 3.12 the frame bar comes with it; the column should list it.
5. **Seams between adjacent wall boxes make the ball snag (ghost collisions).** The curriculum's 3.14 and 3.17 do not mention it, and Manifold measured it at full size. 3.14 must process contacts deepest first, and 3.17's maze must emit merged runs of walls; 3.16 explains why.
6. **The grid walk (Amanatides and Woo) has no concept lesson.** 3.17 needs it for the turrets' sight; the sheet teaches it inside the project lesson as a small idea. It could instead be a section of 3.12 (E's ch. 7 holds both).
7. **The archived 1-01's numbers (reused by 3.12) came from `Math.random()` on a 2D field.** They must be re-measured with the seeded crowd before the lesson quotes them.
8. **E's three printed typos are not located precisely by the readers.** The slab exit (3.9) and the OBB translation index (3.10) are clear; the box-corner helper is placed in 3.15 provisionally. Check the errata before any lesson quotes them.
